You are identifying scientific events in research abstracts.

TASK
Read the full abstract and identify the scientific events whose triggers occur
in the TARGET SENTENCE. For each event, explain the scientific relation using
the relevant argument text and its semantic roles, then assign an event type.
Finish with an inventory of event types and their counts.

The full abstract provides context. An argument may occur in another sentence,
but an event belongs in the answer only when its trigger is in the target
sentence. A target sentence may express no events, one event, or multiple
events, including several events of the same type.

EVENT TYPES AND THEIR ARGUMENT ROLES
Choose a type from the relation expressed by the event and the functions of
its participants. The descriptions below explain the annotation schema; they
are not sentences that the abstract must reproduce word for word. A role may
be absent. Missing roles do not automatically rule out an event type.

PUR — Purpose
A research aim or intended objective: what the work seeks to address or
achieve, possibly in a particular setting or on particular data.
Roles: Aim, Condition, Dataset.
Aim expresses the intended goal; Condition describes the relevant setting;
Dataset identifies the data. Distinguish an intended aim from an observed
finding or achieved effect.

ITT — IntroduceTarget
Introduction or characterization of the abstract research target or task:
what problem, task, or object is being studied.
Roles: Target, Condition, Dataset.
Target is the research target; Condition and Dataset specify its setting and
data. Distinguish introducing the task from stating an aim for addressing it.

RWS — RelatedWorkStep
A procedure, component, or approach used in prior or existing work.
Roles: Subject, Target, BaseComponent, TriedComponent, Condition, Dataset.
Subject is the entity associated with the existing approach; Target is what
it addresses; BaseComponent and TriedComponent describe the components used;
Condition and Dataset provide the setting. Distinguish an existing approach
from an operation within the method presented in this paper.

RWF — RelatedWorkFault
A limitation, deficiency, or difficulty concerning existing work or the
research setting, often motivating the current study.
Roles: Target, Extent, Concern, Fault, Condition, Dataset.
Target is the goal or target implicated in the difficulty; Concern is the
entity under discussion; Fault expresses the limitation; Extent its degree;
Condition and Dataset specify where it applies. Explain the limitation in
relation to the participants actually supported by the text.

PRP — Propose
Introduction of a method, model, framework, resource, or other contribution
as something proposed by the authors.
Roles: Proposer, Content, Target.
Proposer introduces Content for a Target. Distinguish presenting a contribution
from describing one operation performed inside that contribution. The verb
need not literally be "propose".

WKS — WorkStatement
A statement of what the researchers do, report, or study, including the
content, settings, or scope of their work.
Roles: Researcher, Content, Condition, Dataset, Target.
Researcher is associated with the reported Content; Condition, Dataset, and
Target describe its setting, data, and purpose or target. WKS can report an
action performed by the researchers. Decide whether the relation emphasizes
the researchers and their work content, the introduction of a contribution,
the operation of method components, or a finding. Different relations in the
same clause can support different event types.

MDS — MethodStep
An operation or component interaction within the method presented in the
paper, explaining how that method works.
Roles: BaseComponent, TriedComponent, Condition, Dataset, Target.
BaseComponent and TriedComponent describe the components involved in the
operation; Condition, Dataset, and Target specify its setting, data, and
target. Explain the operation and the functions of the available components.
Distinguish this from prior work and from proposing the method as a whole.

FIN — Finding
An observation, finding, or conclusion that the researchers find or
demonstrate, typically from experiments or analysis.
Roles: Finder, Content.
Finder makes or reports the finding; Content is what is found or demonstrated.
Distinguish reporting a finding from carrying out an experiment or stating an
intended goal. A clause can express both a finding event and a separate
comparison or outcome event; classify those relations individually.

CMP — ExperimentCompare
A comparison of systems, methods, results, or other experimental entities.
Roles: Metrics, Arg1, Extent, Result, Arg2, Condition, Dataset.
Arg1 and Arg2 are the compared entities; Result expresses the comparison
outcome or relation; Extent expresses its degree; Metrics identifies the
measure; Condition and Dataset provide the evaluation setting. Comparison
includes superiority, inferiority, or comparable performance. Do not assign
Dataset to a baseline merely because it is a comparison participant.

FAC — OutcomeFact
An outcome relation in which a subject provides, enables, or improves an
object, outcome, or capability for a target.
Roles: Subject, Extent, Object, Target, Condition, Dataset, Reason.
Subject provides or enables Object for Target; Extent describes the degree;
Condition and Dataset specify the setting; Reason gives the stated reason.
Distinguish the outcome relation from the researchers' act of finding it and
from a direct comparison between entities.

HOW TO EXPLAIN AND COUNT EVENTS
For each event, connect the relevant argument text to its role and explain how
the resulting relation supports the chosen type. Mention the event expression
so that different events in the same sentence can be distinguished. Use the
full abstract to resolve context and participants when needed.

Use only argument text and role assignments supported by the abstract. Do not
invent a participant to complete a schema, and do not turn a schema description
into an extra event. Classify each event separately; one clause may support
multiple events when it expresses distinct scientific relations. Different
events may share a trigger when it introduces different propositions or
participant relations. Count the event instances, not the unique trigger
words. Several arguments within one event do not by themselves create extra
events. Decide from the relation and context rather than from a trigger word
alone.

Keep explanations concise and tied to the evidence. Role names and argument
text belong in the explanation; the final inventory contains only event types
and counts. Do not output an event tree or character/token offsets.
